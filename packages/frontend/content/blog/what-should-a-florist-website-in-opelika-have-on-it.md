A florist website in Opelika should show your phone number, address, opening hours, clear photos of your arrangements, and a simple way to order or ask about an order. It should also work well on a phone. Those basics let a customer decide quickly whether you can handle their flowers.

## Why does a florist website matter so much?

For many shoppers, your website is the first look they get at your shop. The Society of American Florists says in its [online sales guidance](https://floraleducationhub.safnow.org/products/online-sales) that a website is where a shopper forms a first impression. It also says florists need a plan for what goes on the site, extra products to offer alongside an order, and work on showing up in local search.

A florist-industry article on [staying profitable](https://safnow.org/2022/07/27/florists-top-tips-for-staying-profitable/) makes a similar point. It calls the website a shop's newest employee and asks two plain questions: can customers find you, and can they order easily? Keep both questions in mind as you decide what goes on the site.

## What pages does a small florist website need?

Most flower shops can cover everything with a handful of pages:

- **Home:** what you sell, where you are, and how to reach you, all visible without scrolling far.
- **Arrangements or shop:** photos of what you make, grouped in a way customers think about, such as birthdays, sympathy, weddings or everyday.
- **Delivery and ordering:** how to place an order, where you deliver, and when to order by.
- **About and contact:** your address, phone, hours, and a short line about the shop.

## Which business details should be easy to find?

Put your phone number, street address and opening hours near the top of the home page and again at the bottom of every page. Customers searching for flowers often need a fast answer, such as whether you are open on Saturday.

Google also lets a local business describe these details in a form that search engines can read. Its [local business documentation](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/structured-data/local-business) lists the address, phone number, opening hours and photos as details you can provide. This is called structured data: a hidden layer of labelled information behind the page. Your web designer can add it, so ask whether it is included.

## How should a florist website show photos of arrangements?

Photos sell flowers, so give them room. Google's [SEO starter guide](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/seo-starter-guide) advises using sharp, clear images, placing them near text that relates to them, and adding short, descriptive alt text. Alt text is a brief written description of a picture. It helps people using screen readers and helps search engines understand the image.

In practice, that means:

- Use a real photo of the arrangement you actually make, in good light.
- Put the name and a short description right beside each photo.
- Write alt text such as "white roses and eucalyptus in a glass vase" rather than "IMG_2041".

## Should a florist website show prices?

Customers usually want a sense of cost before they call. Listing a starting price beside each arrangement, or a clear range for larger pieces like wedding flowers and sympathy work, saves them a phone call and saves you the question. If your prices change with the season, say so on the page.

If you would rather quote custom work individually, say that plainly, and still show prices for the standard items. ## Do I need online ordering on a florist website?

Not necessarily, but customers need an easy way to order. That can be a full online store, a short order form that sends you the details, or a clear phone number with a note on when someone answers. Pick the option you can keep up with every day.

## Should a flower shop website list delivery areas and cutoff times?

Yes. Two of the first things a buyer wants to know are whether you deliver to their address and whether an order placed today will arrive when they need it. Say so in plain words.

- Name the towns you deliver to, such as Opelika, Auburn, Columbus or Phenix City, if those are the places you actually serve.
- State the same-day order cutoff time.
- Explain how you handle delivery fees, if you charge them, and what happens for hospitals or funeral homes.
- Say whether customers can pick up in the shop.

Keep this on its own section of the page rather than buried in a paragraph.

## Does a florist website need to work on a phone?

Yes, and not only for convenience. Google's [mobile-first indexing guidance](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/mobile/mobile-sites-mobile-first-indexing) says it builds its search listings from the mobile version of a page, so the content should match what appears on a desktop. For a florist, that means your hours, phone number and photos must all show on a phone, not just on a computer.

## What is a good next step?

Open your current website, or a competitor's, on your phone and run through this list:

1. Can you see the phone number, address and hours without searching?
2. Do the arrangement photos load clearly, with a name beside each?
3. Can you tell where the shop delivers and when to order by?
4. Can you place an order, or ask about one, in a couple of taps?

Write down every "no". That list is your content plan for the new or rebuilt site.
