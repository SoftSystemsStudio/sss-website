For most restaurants and coffee shops, the menu should be a regular web page, with a PDF kept only as an optional download for people who want to print it. [Usability research](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/pdf-unfit-for-human-consumption/) finds PDFs slow and awkward on phones, and that is where most of your customers will look.

## Why is a PDF menu bad on a phone?

A PDF is built like a printed page. It keeps a fixed layout, so a phone has to shrink a whole sheet of paper onto a small screen. That is why people end up pinching and zooming to read a single line.

The Nielsen Norman Group, a well-known usability research firm, [reports that PDFs are slow to load and awkward on mobile](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/pdf-unfit-for-human-consumption/), with pinching, zooming and tiny text. For a menu, that means someone standing in a parking lot or sitting on a couch has to work to find the price of a sandwich. Some of them will give up and look at the next place instead.

A web page works differently. The text reflows to fit the width of the screen, so it is readable without zooming.

## Can Google read a PDF menu?

Yes, sometimes. Google has said it can [generally index the text in a PDF if that text can be copied](https://developers.google.com/search/blog/2011/09/pdfs-in-google-search-results). Indexing just means Google adds the content to the records it searches through. The same post says Google does not index images inside PDFs.

That difference matters for menus. If your PDF was exported from a document where you can select the words, the text is there to be read. If it is a photo or a scan of a printed menu, the words are an image, and Google does not index images inside PDFs.

So a PDF menu is not invisible to Google. But being findable is only half the job. The person who finds it still has to be able to read it on a phone.

## What do web teams recommend instead?

Two outside sources point the same way:

- A US government web team writes that [most PDF content would be better as a standard HTML page](https://digital.gov/2024/12/31/reduce-remove-remediate-pdfs-and-government-websites), with PDFs kept only for specific, valid uses. (HTML is the ordinary format web pages are written in.) That advice is aimed at government sites, but the reasoning carries over to a menu.
- A university accessibility guide says content meant to be read online, or [content that changes often, should default to HTML](https://www.umassd.edu/accessibility/digital-accessibility/documents/pdfs/), with PDFs used only when a document format is truly needed.

A menu is both: something people read on screen, and something that changes. That puts it firmly on the web page side.

## Can I keep a PDF menu for people who want to print it?

Yes. You do not have to choose one or the other. The usability research above suggests [an HTML page that leads to a printable PDF](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/pdf-unfit-for-human-consumption/) as a workable setup.

In practice, that looks like this:

1. The menu lives on a normal page of your website, readable on any phone.
2. Near the top, there is a clear link such as "Download printable menu (PDF)."
3. The link says it is a PDF, so nobody is surprised by what opens.

Catering customers, office managers and people planning a group order are the ones most likely to want that printable copy.

## What makes a menu web page easy to use on a phone?

A web page is not automatically good. A few basics make the difference:

- **Real text, not a photo of a menu.** Text can be read aloud by screen readers, resized by the customer and picked up by search.
- **Short sections with clear headings,** such as Breakfast, Sandwiches, Drinks and Sides.
- **Buttons and links that are easy to tap.** Google's web.dev guidance recommends [touch targets of about 48 pixels with about 8 pixels of space between them](https://web.dev/articles/accessible-tap-targets). A pixel is one tiny dot on the screen, so in plain terms: make your "Call," "Directions" and "Order online" buttons big enough for a thumb and spaced so people do not hit the wrong one.
- **Your hours and phone number near the menu,** so a hungry customer does not have to go looking for them.

## How do I check my menu on my own phone?

This takes about five minutes:

1. Open your website on your phone, using mobile data rather than your restaurant's wifi.
2. Find the menu the way a stranger would, starting from the home page.
3. Count how many taps it takes and how long it takes to load.
4. Try to read an item and its price without zooming. If you have to pinch, note it.
5. Tap your phone number and your directions link. Make sure they work and are easy to hit.
6. Ask a friend or regular customer to try the same thing and tell you where they got stuck.

## How do I update my menu when prices or items change?

This is where a PDF costs you the most time. To change one price, you have to edit the original file, export a new PDF and upload it again, and you have to make sure the old copy is gone everywhere it was linked or saved.

With a menu web page, you change the text on that page. There is one version, and it is the current one. As the university guide above says, content that changes often belongs in HTML.
