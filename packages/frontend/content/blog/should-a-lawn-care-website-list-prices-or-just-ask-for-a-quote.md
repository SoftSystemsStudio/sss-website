For most lawn care businesses, the safest answer is the middle: show a starting price or a few sample lawn sizes with prices, then offer an exact quote once you know the yard. Leaving prices out entirely can [send visitors to a competitor's site](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/show-price/), so give people something to compare.

## Do customers leave a website that doesn't show prices?

Some do. Nielsen Norman Group, a research firm that studies how people use websites, reported that in its usability testing people [often left for competitors' sites when prices were missing](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/show-price/). Their work focused on business buyers rather than homeowners, so treat it as a strong hint, not a rule for every lawn customer.

There is also a trust side. The same firm notes that when a price varies, [a starting price or a range](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/b2b-trust-from-b2c/) is better than nothing, because hiding it can make a business look like it has something to hide.

A homeowner in Opelika or Phenix City may be comparing several companies in one sitting. The site that gives them a ballpark is easier to consider than one that only says "call for a quote."

## Can I show a starting price instead of exact prices?

Yes, and for lawn care it is often the most practical choice. Your price depends on things you can only judge by seeing the property: lawn size, slopes, fences, how overgrown it is, how often you visit. A starting price or a range is honest about that.

Two habits keep it useful:

- Say what the starting price covers, such as "weekly mowing for a small, flat lawn."
- Say what moves the price up, so nobody feels surprised later.

## How do I show example lawn prices?

If a single starting price feels too vague, use sample jobs. Nielsen Norman Group suggests that [pricing a handful of typical situations](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/show-prices-for-common-scenarios/) helps people during early research when a full price list would be too complicated. A lawn care writer on the Landscape Leadership blog describes a similar approach: [example programs at a sample lawn size](https://www.landscapeleadership.com/blog/why-how-to-address-cost-of-services-on-website-lawn-care-landscaping), with an exact quote after the visitor submits a form.

In practice, that could look like this:

- Small lawn, weekly mowing: your price
- Medium lawn, weekly mowing and edging: your price
- Large lawn, every-other-week mowing: your price
- Seasonal clean-up for an average yard: your price

Under the list, add one line: "Every yard is different, so request a quote for an exact price." That keeps the examples honest and still gives people a reason to contact you.

## What should a lawn care quote form ask for?

Ask only what you need to give a first answer. A shorter form is easier to finish, and you can collect the rest on the phone or at the property. A sensible short list:

1. Name and best phone number or email
2. Property address, or at least the street and town
3. Services wanted (mowing, edging, clean-up, other)
4. Rough lawn size, if the customer knows it, with "not sure" as an acceptable answer
5. A box for anything else you should know, such as a gate code or a dog in the yard

After the form, tell people what happens next and how soon you usually reply, but only promise a timeframe you can keep.

## Do I need online booking, or is a quote form enough?

If you price each yard after seeing it, a quote form fits how you already work. Online booking makes more sense when the price is fixed and the job is simple, because the customer can pick a date without a conversation first.

Many lawn businesses start with a quote form and change later if they find they are answering the same simple requests again and again. Booking tools are an extra feature, so it is reasonable to leave them out of a first site.

## What should a lawn care website have besides prices?

Prices are one piece. Visitors also look for the basics:

- Your phone number at the top of every page, easy to tap on a phone
- A plain list of the services you offer
- The towns you serve, written as real place names
- Photos of your own work
- A short explanation of how quotes work

## Do the prices I publish have to be accurate?

Yes. The Federal Trade Commission's guide for small businesses says that [any price you advertise has to be truthful](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/advertising-faqs-guide-small-business). Conditions that affect the cost should be disclosed near the price, and the amount you actually charge should match what you advertised.

For a lawn business, that means a "starting at" price should be one you really charge for the job described, and any limits (lawn size, number of visits, extra fees for overgrown yards) should sit right beside it, not on a separate page.

## Your next step

Write down the three jobs people ask you about most, and a price or starting price for each one. Then add one sentence for what changes the number. That is the content for a pricing section you can show on a new website, and it also gives you a ready answer the next time someone calls and asks, "How much do you charge?"
