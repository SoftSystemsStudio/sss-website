A house cleaning business website should cost what it takes to build the pages and tools you actually need, plus a small yearly amount to keep it running. Think of it as two budgets: a one-time build and recurring costs such as your domain name. Here is how to size each one.

## What goes into a cleaning business website budget?

The Small Business Administration lists making a website among the [advertising and marketing expenses to include when you calculate startup costs](https://www.sba.gov/business-guide/plan-your-business). So the website belongs in your budget next to your other costs of getting started, not as an afterthought.

Split it into two lines:

- **One-time:** the design and build of the site, including writing and photos.
- **Recurring:** the domain name, plus whatever keeps the site online and up to date.

Seeing both lines before you hire anyone makes quotes easier to compare, because one cheap build can come with expensive upkeep, and the reverse.

## What makes a cleaning website cost more or less?

One developer's [budget breakdown for cleaning company sites](https://dev.to/amanhstu/what-a-cleaning-company-website-really-costs-a-developers-budget-breakdown-45ho) points to a handful of things that move the price. It is one person's view, not a local price list, but the factors match what you will hear from most builders:

- **Number of pages.** More pages mean more writing, layout and testing.
- **Quote or booking tools.** A form that sends you a request is simpler than a system that checks availability and takes bookings.
- **Template or custom design.** A template is a pre-made layout filled with your content. Custom design is drawn from scratch and usually costs more.
- **Photos.** Photos of your team, your work and the area you serve take effort to gather, and they add to the job.

When you ask for a quote, ask which of these four it assumes. Two quotes that look far apart often assume different answers.

## What pages should a cleaning business website have?

Most cleaning companies can start small. A sensible first list:

1. **Home:** what you clean, where, and how to reach you.
2. **Services:** regular house cleaning, deep cleaning, move-out cleaning, office cleaning, whatever you actually offer.
3. **Service area:** the towns you cover, such as Smiths Station, Columbus, Phenix City, Auburn and Opelika.
4. **About:** who you are, so a stranger feels comfortable letting you into their home.
5. **Contact or quote request:** your phone number and a short form.

Because page count affects price, this is the easiest place to hold the budget down. You can add pages later once you know what customers ask about.

## Do I need online booking or just a quote form?

For most small cleaning businesses, a quote form is enough to start. Cleaning prices usually depend on the size of the home and the type of job, so many owners want to see the details before giving a number anyway.

Booking tools are one of the cost drivers in the breakdown above, so they are worth adding only if you will use them every week. If you are unsure whether to show prices on the site at all, the same question comes up for another trade in [should a lawn care website list prices or ask for a quote](/blog/should-a-lawn-care-website-list-prices-or-just-ask-for-a-quote).

## What does it cost each year to keep a small business website running?

The clearest yearly cost is your domain name, the web address customers type in. It is registered through a registrar (a company that sells domain names) and has to be renewed regularly. According to the SBA, you keep the name [only as long as you keep renewing it](https://www.sba.gov/business-guide/launch-your-business/choose-your-business-name), so it is a recurring cost to plan for.

The first-year price is not always the renewal price. ICANN notes that [registrars set their own renewal prices, which can differ from the first-year fee](https://www.icann.org/resources/pages/domain-name-renewal-expiration-faqs-2018-12-07-en), so read the renewal terms before you register.

Beyond the domain, ask any builder what is included for hosting (the service that keeps the site online), security updates and small text changes. Get the answer in writing, with a yearly total.

## Is a DIY website builder enough for a cleaning company?

It can be, for a simple site. A do-it-yourself builder swaps money for your time, and cleaning owners tend to have very little spare time. Before you commit, check three things: what the renewal price is after the first term, whether you can use your own domain name, and whether you can take your content with you if you leave.

If you would rather not spend evenings on layout, paying someone for a small, focused site is a reasonable choice. Either way, the page list above is the same.

## Who should own the domain name for my cleaning business?

You should. The registrant is the person or business that holds the rights to the domain, and [fees, transfers and renewals follow the agreement with the registrar](https://www.icann.org/resources/pages/domain-name-registration-process-2023-11-02-en).

The catch is that a developer or other third party who sets up the domain for you may end up listed as the registrant instead of your business. ICANN suggests [checking with the registrar to see who the registrant of record is](https://www.icann.org/resources/pages/faqs-84-2012-02-25-en). If the answer is not you, ask for it to be corrected.

## Your next step

Before you ask for any quote, write down two things: the pages from the list above that you really need, and whether you want a quote form or full booking. Then ask every builder, or builder platform, for the one-time price and the yearly total, and ask who will be listed as the registrant of your domain. With those answers on paper, the quotes become much easier to compare.
