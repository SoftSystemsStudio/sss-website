Yes, get it in writing, but for a small one-page site that does not have to mean a long legal document. A [short letter of agreement or a clear email can work](http://webdesignlaw.com/), as long as it states the scope, price, deadline, and who owns the site, domain and accounts.

This is general information for business owners, not legal advice. If your project is large or unusual, ask a lawyer to look over the agreement.

## Is an email agreement enough for a small website job?

Often, yes. One guide to web design contracts says small jobs can use a [short letter of agreement or an email that spells out scope, price and deadline](http://webdesignlaw.com/), and that a written agreement is better than an oral one.

The key word is *clear*. An email that says "sounds good, let's get started" is not an agreement about anything. An email that lists what you are getting, what it costs, when it is due, and what you own at the end is.

The simplest way to do it: the designer sends a short written proposal, and you reply with "I agree to the above" and the date. Keep both messages somewhere you can find them later.

## What should be in writing before you pay a deposit?

A [web design contract checklist](https://61web.com.au/blog/web-design-contract-checklist/) covers these items, and they fit a one-page site well:

- **Scope:** what the page will include, such as your services, phone number, hours, a contact form and photos.
- **Timeline:** when you will get a first draft and when the site goes live.
- **Revisions:** how many rounds of changes are included.
- **Total price and payment schedule:** what you pay, when, and what each payment covers.
- **Ownership:** who owns the site, the domain and the accounts.
- **Handover and support:** what you receive when the site launches and what help is included afterward.

Watch for vague wording in that last item. One contract guide warns that a phrase like ["ongoing support" with no hours or time limit](https://kreativagroup.com/post/web-design-contract-what-to-include-and-why-it-matters) is a problem. It should also address hosting, domain ownership and maintenance after launch. If support is included, write down for how long and for what. If it costs extra, say so. Many designers offer a monthly plan for this, and the terms should be just as specific.

## Who owns my website after I pay the designer?

Do not assume it is you. The U.S. Copyright Office explains that the person who creates a work is [normally its author, and work made for hire is a narrow exception](https://www.copyright.gov/circs/circ30.pdf). A law firm that writes about outsourced marketing work makes the practical point: independent contractors such as web designers [own what they create by default](https://www.aberlawfirm.com/who-owns-your-marketing-content/), and a contract should include an outright assignment of copyright to the client.

In plain terms, paying the final invoice does not automatically transfer the design to you. A sentence like this closes the gap: "Upon full payment, the designer assigns to the client all rights in the website design and content created for this project." Ask for wording along those lines, and ask what happens with any photos, fonts or templates the designer licensed from someone else.

## Should the domain be registered in your business name?

Yes. Your domain is your web address, such as yourbusiness.com. It is registered through a company called a registrar, and the person or business listed as the owner on that registration is called the Registered Name Holder.

That listing matters. ICANN's transfer policy says the [Registered Name Holder is the only party who can approve or deny a domain transfer](https://www.icann.org/en/contracted-parties/accredited-registrars/resources/domain-name-transfers/policy). If the designer registered the domain under their own name, moving it later depends on their cooperation.

The fix is simple:

1. Register the domain yourself, with your own account and your business name and email.
2. Or, if the designer does it for you, confirm in writing that you will be listed as the owner and receive the login.
3. Do the same for hosting and any other accounts the site depends on.

## What happens to my site if I want to switch designers later?

This is the question the earlier answers prepare you for. If you own the design, hold the domain, and have the account logins, switching is a handover rather than a negotiation. The checklist above lists [ownership of the site, domain and accounts](https://61web.com.au/blog/web-design-contract-checklist/) and post-launch handover as things to settle up front.

Before you sign, ask for one line about what you will receive if the relationship ends: the site files, the logins, and any content you supplied. It is a fair request, and a designer who is comfortable with it is easier to trust.

## Your next step

Before you pay anyone a deposit, write five sentences in an email: what you expect to receive, the price, the date, who owns the design, and whose name will be on the domain. Send it to the designer and ask them to confirm or correct it. Their reply is your agreement, and the way they respond tells you a lot about how the job will go.
