Usually not at the start. A contractor website does not need a separate page for each town it serves unless a town page has something real to say that is only true of that town. Otherwise, one strong service-area page that names your towns is the better place to begin.

## Is one page per city doorway spam in Google's eyes?

It can be, depending on how the pages are made. Google's [spam policies](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials/spam-policies) include a section on "doorway abuse." It describes pages built to rank for specific cities that are nearly identical to each other, and pages whose main job is to funnel visitors on to another page.

So the risky version is easy to picture: you write one page about roofing, copy it five times, and swap in Phenix City, Opelika, Auburn and so on. That is the pattern the policy describes. A town page isn't forbidden. A town page that exists only to carry a town name is the problem.

## When does a town deserve its own page?

A town page earns its place when it has something that is only true of that town. If you could delete the town name and paste in a different one without anything else changing, the page isn't ready. One [web design guide on city pages](https://www.webdesignservices.net/separate-website-page-every-city-you-serve/) makes the same point: when only the heading changes, one strong service-area page is the better start.

A [practitioner thread on SitePoint](https://www.sitepoint.com/community/t/local-service-pages-separate-city-pages-or-one-main-service-area-page/491935) reaches a similar conclusion. Put your effort into one solid main page. Give a town its own page only when you have material from that town, such as work you have finished there, feedback from customers there, or knowledge of the place. The remaining towns can go in a short list.

Before you add a town page, ask yourself:

- Do I have finished jobs in this town that I can describe or show?
- Have customers here said anything about my work that I can quote with their permission?
- Is there something about working here that I can explain, such as how I schedule or what I handle differently?
- Would a homeowner in this town learn something from this page that they would not learn from my main page?

If the answer to all four is no, the town belongs in a list, not on a page of its own.

## How many towns should I list on my service area page?

List the towns you actually work in and would be glad to get a call from. Your site should [plainly describe the area you serve](https://daviswebdesign.com/resources/service-business-show-service-area-on-website/), and a visitor should be able to tell within a few seconds whether you come to them.

There is no magic number. A contractor near Smiths Station might reasonably name Smiths Station, Phenix City, Columbus, Auburn and Opelika. If you rarely travel to one of those, leave it off. A long list of places you would not really go makes the page harder to read and sets the wrong expectation.

If you also want to know whether to name towns across the state line, the earlier post on [whether a Columbus, GA business site should mention Phenix City and Smiths Station](/blog/should-a-columbus-ga-business-website-name-phenix-city-and-smiths-station) covers that question.

## What goes on one strong service-area page?

The same Davis Web Design article notes that a page for a particular community has to give people something useful. Repeating a template with a different place name does not do that. A single page that covers your whole area avoids the problem, because it never pretends to be about one place.

A good service-area page usually has:

1. A short opening that says what you do and where you do it.
2. The list of towns you serve, written as normal text people can read.
3. The services you offer across that area.
4. A clear way to reach you: a phone number you answer and a simple contact option.
5. Any real detail that helps a customer decide, such as how far you travel or how quickly you can usually start.

Keep each item truthful and specific to your business. Do not pad the page to make it look bigger.

## Can a one-page site cover several towns?

Yes. A one-page site can include a section that names the towns you work in, next to your services and contact details. For many trades that is enough while the business is small, because the goal is to help a customer confirm you cover their area and then call.

If you later find that one town has real material behind it, you can add a page for it then. Starting small does not close that door.

## What should you do next?

Write down every town you work in. Next to each one, note whether you have finished jobs, customer comments or local knowledge that is specific to it. Towns with nothing next to them go into the list on your service-area page. A town with plenty can be considered for a page of its own later, once you have the material to fill it.
